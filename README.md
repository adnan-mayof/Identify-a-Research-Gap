# Step 1 — Identify a Research Gap by First Reviewing Existing Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses

## 👩‍🔬 Story 1 — “Maya Has a Question”

Maya is interested in AI-powered learning technologies.

She thinks:

> “AI is everywhere in education now. But does it actually improve student learning?”

She is excited to start a meta-analysis.

But her mentor stops her.

**Mentor:** “Before you start searching for studies, there’s something you need to do first.”

**Maya:** “What?”

**Mentor:** “Find out what researchers have already discovered about this topic. Look for existing systematic reviews, scoping reviews, and meta-analyses.”

**Maya:** “And then I just summarize them?”

**Mentor:** “Not exactly. You need to compare them and look for what is still missing.”

Maya opens several previous reviews.

**Mentor:** “Use the **PRISMA 2020 checklist** to help you examine how these reviews were reported. Pay attention to their objectives, eligibility criteria, information sources, search strategies, included studies, synthesis methods, and limitations.”

Maya starts making notes.

**Maya:** “So I should check what they covered and what they didn't?”

**Mentor:** “Exactly. Look for gaps. Maybe previous researchers didn't examine certain features of the AI technologies. Maybe they didn't examine important moderators. Maybe their reviews are outdated. Or maybe their findings don't agree.”

Maya creates a comparison table for the previous reviews.

**Maya:** “I think I'm starting to see some things that haven't been answered yet.”

**Mentor:** “Good. Now you can begin asking whether those gaps justify a new review.”

**What should Maya do first?**

---

## 🎯 The First Step

A systematic review should begin with a **research problem or evidence gap**, not simply with the decision to conduct a meta-analysis.

Start by reading the existing literature and identifying **systematic reviews, scoping reviews, and meta-analyses on the same or closely related topic**.

The purpose of this step is to understand the existing evidence base and identify a **clear research gap**.

> **PRISMA (2020), Items 3–4 — Rationale and Objectives:** Use the existing evidence base to establish why the review is needed and to formulate the review objectives.

Do not simply list previous reviews. Create a structured summary of each review and compare them with one another.

### 🔎 What Should Maya Look For?

Maya's mentor gives her a checklist:

* What has already been studied?
* What populations have been studied?
* What interventions have been studied?
* What outcomes have been examined?
* What moderators have been examined?
* What methodological approaches have been used?
* What theoretical or conceptual frameworks have been used?
* How were the theoretical or conceptual frameworks used in the previous reviews?
* Did the theoretical frameworks inform the research questions, intervention mechanisms, or moderator selection?
* What theoretical frameworks appear relevant to the proposed research problem?
* Are there theoretical gaps in the existing reviews?
* What limitations remain?
* How many studies were included?
* What years did the included studies cover?
* What were the main findings?
* Are there important inconsistencies between reviews?
* Are previous reviews outdated?
* Have new technologies or interventions emerged?
* Have new populations or outcomes emerged?
* Have important studies been published since previous reviews?
* **Is a new systematic review or meta-analysis justified?**

### 💡 What Does Maya Notice?

Maya steps back from the table.

**Maya:** “I can see the gaps much more clearly now.”

**Mentor:** “What do you see?”

**Maya:** “The reviews don't all examine the same populations, interventions, or outcomes.”

**Mentor:** “Good. What else?”

**Maya:** “Some of them don't examine the **features of the AI technologies**. Others don't examine important **moderators** that might explain why the effects differ.”

**Mentor:** “And what about theory?”

**Maya:** “Theory seems to be missing or only loosely connected to the analysis. The previous reviews don't consistently use theory to explain the mechanisms or select moderators.”

**Mentor:** “Exactly. Now you're not simply saying, ‘There are previous reviews.’ You can explain **what they covered, what they missed, and why another review may be needed.**”

Maya looks at her table again.

**Maya:** “So this comparison is what helps me build the case for my own review.”

**Mentor:** “Exactly.”

### 🧠 Maya's Takeaway

> **Don't ask only: “What did previous reviews find?”**
>
> Also ask: **“What did they not examine, and what has changed since they were published?”**

That is where Maya may find the **research gap** that will shape her new systematic review and meta-analysis.
